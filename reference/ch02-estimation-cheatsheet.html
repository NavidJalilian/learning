2.1 The byte ladder Quest 2.1 →

A back-of-the-envelope estimate is a rough number built from a thought experiment plus a few well-known performance figures (the idea comes from Jeff Dean at Google). The interviewer wants to see you reason about scale. Precision is not the point.

PowerExactRoughlyUnitDrift
2101,0241 thousand (103)KB+2.4%
2201,048,5761 million (106)MB+4.9%
2301,073,741,8241 billion (109)GB+7.4%
240≈ 1.10 × 10121 trillion (1012)TB+10.0%
250≈ 1.13 × 10151 quadrillion (1015)PB+12.6%

Pattern: every +10 on the exponent = ×~1,000 = three more zeros = one unit up. The 2.4% error compounds per rung, but even at PB it's only 12.6%: the right order of magnitude.

Count the zeros
  1. Write each number as small × 10n
  2. Multiply the small numbers, add the exponents
  3. Snap the exponent to a unit: 3 K · 6 M · 9 G · 12 T · 15 P
300 M users × 1 KB = 3×108 × 103 = 3×1011 B = 300 GB. Percentages too: 10% = 10−1.
Book tip: round and approximate
99,987 ÷ 9.1 → 100,000 ÷ 10 = 10,000 in a second. A dropped zero (10×) is far worse than any rounding error.
Book tip: label your units
"5" could be 5 KB or 5 MB: a 1,000× slip. Write 5 MB. (DAU, QPS carry the unit in the name.)
Day shortcut (not the book's)
86,400 s ≈ 105: 1 M events/day ≈ 11.6/s (≈ 10/s by shortcut); 1 B/day ≈ 11,600/s. The shortcut runs ~14% low; say so if you use it.

KB vs KiB: strictly KB/MB/GB are decimal and KiB/MiB/GiB binary. A "1 TB" drive shows as ~931 "GB" (really GiB) on Windows. Say "I'll treat 210 as 1,000" and move on.

"300 million is 3 × 108 and 1 KB is 103 bytes, so that's 3 × 1011 bytes, 300 GB. I'm treating 210 as a thousand, which is a few percent off: fine for an estimate."

The five conclusions

  1. Memory is fast, disk is slow. Memory read vs disk seek: 100,000×.
  2. Avoid disk seeks. 100 random reads = 1 s; one seek + 1 MB sequential = 40 ms.
  3. Simple compression is fast (microseconds per KB).
  4. Compress before sending over the internet: 100 KB costs ~1 ms of CPU against a 150 ms trip.
  5. Regions are far apart. Moving data between data centers takes time: 5 sequential cross-region calls = 750 ms; batch them or keep a copy in-region.
Human time (×2 billion)
L1 = 1 s · memory = 3.3 min · same-DC trip = 12 days · disk seek = 7.6 months · ocean round trip = 9½ years.
Today (rough)
SSD random read ~0.1 ms (100× faster than a seek); NVMe reads GB/s; DC links 10–100 Gbps. RAM and caches barely moved. Light in fiber needs ~90 ms CA↔NL and back (9,000 km at 200,000 km/s): physics, not hardware.

"A memory read is about 100 ns and a disk seek about 10 ms, so memory wins by ~100,000× for random access. Even a cache one hop away in the same data center only adds ~0.5 ms. Cross-region calls cost ~150 ms each, so I'd batch them or keep data in-region. SSDs shrank the disk gap since 2010; the speed of light didn't move."

One formula
downtime = (1 − A) × period
Year = 525,960 min (365.25 days); month ≈ 43,830 min. Each extra nine = ÷10 downtime. 99.95% = half of 99.9%'s.
Series (all must work): multiply
3 × 99.9% → 0.999³ ≈ 99.7% (~26 h/yr). 4 → 99.6%, 5 → 99.5%. Shortcut: small down-percentages add.
Parallel (any one works)
1 − (1 − A)n: two 99% boxes → 99.99%, only if they fail independently. Shared rack, zone, deploy or config → closer to a single box.
SLI · SLO · SLA
SLI = what you measure. SLO = your target for it. SLA = the contract; cloud SLAs (99.9%+) pay service credits, not your losses. Set SLO tighter than SLA. Error budget = 1 − SLO (99.9% → 43.8 min/month); spend it on releases, freeze when it's gone.

"I'd target three to four nines: 99.9% is about 43 minutes down a month, 99.99% about 4.4. Each nine is 10× less downtime and a lot more cost. Our availability can't beat the product of our hard dependencies, so I'd add redundancy where it counts, in different zones so the failures stay independent, and run an SLO tighter than the SLA."

2.4 Tweet math: the book's worked example Quest 2.4 →

Five written assumptions
  • 300 M monthly active users (MAU)
  • 50% use it daily
  • 2 tweets per daily user per day
  • 10% of tweets carry media
  • Keep data 5 years
QPS chain
  • DAU = 300 M × 50% = 150 M
  • Tweets/day = 150 M × 2 = 300 M
  • Write QPS = 300 M ÷ 86,400 ≈ ~3,500
  • Peak = 2 × QPS ≈ ~7,000
Storage chain
  • Tweet: id 64 B + text 140 B + media 1 MB (if any)
  • Media/day = 150 M × 2 × 10% × 1 MB = 30 TB
  • 5 years = 30 TB × 365 × 5 ≈ 55 PB
  • × 3 replicas ≈ 165 PB (not in the book)
If this changes……this moves
DAU, tweets per userQPS, peak and storage, in proportion (×2 → ×2)
Media share, media size, years, replicasStorage only, in proportion
Peak factorPeak QPS only. Spikes bunch the same tweets; they add no bytes
Text length (140 → 280)Text storage (~61 → ~100 GB/day). Media unchanged; text stays tiny

Text is ~61 GB/day (300 M × 204 B), about 500× smaller than media, which is why the book counts only media. Bonus: 30 TB/day ≈ 350 MB/s of uploads (~2.8 Gbps).

"Assumptions first: 300 million MAU, half daily, 2 tweets each, 10% with ~1 MB media, kept 5 years. That's 150 million DAU, 300 million tweets a day, ~3,500 per second and ~7,000 at a 2× peak. Media is 30 TB a day, ~55 PB over five years, ~165 PB with three copies. Text is noise next to that."

BOSS · The Estimation Gauntlet (mock interview) Boss quest →

Prompt: "Estimate the scale of a Twitter-like service." Ten minutes on the clock (scope 2 · plan 1 · crunch 4 · follow-ups 2 · wrap-up 1). Ask for the inputs first: users, daily share, posts, reads, media, retention. Don't ask about databases or languages yet: they change none of the numbers.

The final "design": each estimate after the numbers it uses. Circled digits = the 8-step order.

Sanity check: run it backwards. 3,500/s × 86,400 ≈ 300 M posts/day, which matches 150 M × 2.

Follow-ups to expect
  • Media 10% → 20%: storage ×2 → ~110 PB
  • World Cup spike: 2× is a guess; plan for bursts (queues, autoscaling)
  • 10 sequential cross-ocean calls: 10 × 150 ms = 1.5 s → batch
  • Cache in memory or on disk: 100 ns vs 10 ms, and 60 GB fits
  • 3 services at 99.9% in a chain: ~99.7%
How the close is graded
  • Restate assumptions: every input, round
  • Headline numbers: rounded, labeled, peaks included
  • Name the shakiest input (the peak factor)
  • Turn numbers into design moves
  • Too vague and too precise both lose points

"Assumptions: 300 million MAU, half daily, 2 posts and 10 timeline loads a day, 10% with ~1 MB media, 5-year retention. That's 150 million DAU: ~3,500 writes a second, 7,000 at peak; ~17,000 reads a second, 35,000 at peak. Media is 30 TB a day, ~55 PB over five years, ~165 PB with three replicas, so it goes in object storage behind a CDN. A day of tweet text is ~60 GB, which fits in an in-memory cache. At ~1,000 requests a second per server, I need about 50 app servers. My shakiest number is the peak factor, so I'd plan for bursts with queues and autoscaling."

Honest caveats

  • The latency table is ~2010 hardware. SSDs and faster networks changed the disk and network rows a lot; RAM and the ocean round trip barely moved. Versions of the list also disagree (Dean's 2009 slides say 25 ns for a mutex and 20 ms for 1 MB from disk). Treat every entry as an order of magnitude.
  • 2× peak is a rule of thumb. In August 2013 Twitter hit 143,199 tweets/s against a typical ~5,700: about 25×. Say the factor out loud as an assumption.
  • The book's numbers aren't real Twitter data, and its 64-byte tweet ID is generous (Snowflake IDs are 64 bits = 8 bytes). Tweets allow 280 characters since 2017, and UTF-8 characters can take up to 4 bytes. None of it changes the media-dominated answer.
  • The gauntlet's extra inputs are interviewer-given practice numbers: 10 timeline loads a day, ~200 B per cached tweet, ~1,000 requests/s per server. Real per-server throughput ranges from hundreds to tens of thousands of requests a second. "A few thousand writes/s per DB primary" is a rough guide, not a fact.
  • Redundancy math assumes independent failures. Correlated failures (one rack, one region, one bad deploy) make real availability worse than the formula.
  • 3 full replicas is the simple answer. Big blob stores often use erasure coding for similar safety at ~1.5× the raw size.
  • Series/parallel math, SLO/SLI and error budgets aren't in the book chapter. They come from Google's SRE book and are common senior-level follow-ups.
