Quest 11.2 · Design a News Feed System

Push, Pull, or Both

Ana posts a photo. When does it land in her friends' feeds: the moment she posts, or the moment they open the app? That one choice decides whether a pop star with 40 million followers can melt your servers.

🎯

Your win today: you can explain fanout on write and fanout on read with two pros and two cons each, say why celebrities and inactive users break the push model, and defend the hybrid: push for most users, pull for the very popular.

1

Push: deliver at write time

Fanout on write

In Quest 11.1 you drew the publishing flow: the web servers hand each new post to the post service, which saves it, and, side by side, to a fanout service, which delivers it to friends. Fanout means "spread one thing out to many places". The book's deep dive asks when that spreading happens.[1]

Option one is fanout on write, also called the push model. Each user has a ready-made feed: a short list of post IDs kept in a cache (fast memory). The moment Ana publishes, her post ID is pushed into every friend's list. The feed is precomputed: built before anyone asks for it.

The book calls this the "hotkey problem". Strictly, the pain here is write amplification: one post becomes 40 million writes, and the fanout queue backs up for minutes. A hot key (one single key everyone hammers) is a related but separate issue. Either way, push breaks down for accounts with huge followings.
2

Pull: build it when asked

Fanout on read

Option two flips it. In fanout on read, the pull model, nothing is copied when someone posts. The post is saved once, in the author's own list. When Bo opens his home page, the system goes and fetches recent posts from everyone he follows and combines them, on demand.[1]

Two words you'll need. Fan-in: gather from many sources into one place. Merge: combine lists that are each already sorted into one sorted list (here: newest first).

The hybrid dial

Push for most, pull for the famous

The book's fix takes the best of both. Use push for the vast majority of users, so feeds load fast. For celebrities, or anyone with a huge number of friends or followers, don't fan out their posts. Instead, their followers pull those posts at read time and merge them into the feed that was already precomputed.[1]

First, feel the numbers. These are our own illustrative assumptions. The book doesn't do estimation for this chapter.

② One news page has 50 million followers and posts once. With pure push, how many feed writes does that single post cause?

Plus: spread the load. The book also suggests consistent hashing (the hash ring from Quest 6.2) to spread requests and data more evenly across cache nodes, which eases hotspots.[1] Honest limit: a ring spreads many keys evenly, but it cannot split one very hot key. Real systems copy hot items to several nodes or cache them locally.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

Twitter publicly described a hybrid like the book's in 2012. Tweets were fanned out into per-user timelines held in Redis, and tweets from very high-follower accounts were merged in at read time instead.[2]

The research version goes finer. Feeding Frenzy (SIGMOD 2010) picks push or pull for each producer–consumer pair, based on how often one posts and the other reads. A busy reader following a quiet poster gets push. A rare reader following a chatty poster gets pull.[3]

Two more honest notes. With the book's 5,000-friend cap, pure push is bounded (at most 5,000 writes per post); the celebrity problem really bites when followers are unlimited, as on Twitter or Instagram. And consistent hashing (Karger et al., 1997)[4] spreads many keys well but can't split one hot key.
