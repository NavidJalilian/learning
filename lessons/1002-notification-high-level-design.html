1

Version 1: one box

The naive design and its three cracks

In Quest 10.1 you met the three roads to a user: push (through Apple's APNs[4] or Google's FCM[5]), SMS and email. Now, who asks for a notification to be sent?

The book calls them services: anything that wants to tell a user something. It might be a micro-service, a cron job (a task that runs on a timer), or a whole distributed system. Think of a billing service that emails "your payment is due", or a shop that texts "your package arrives tomorrow".[1]

The book's first sketch puts one notification server in the middle. It offers an API (calls other programs can make) to the services. It also builds the payload (the message in the exact format each provider wants) for APNs, FCM, the SMS provider and the email provider. Everything lives inside it: the database, the cache, the HTML email renderer and the sender.

The book's three problems with version 1. Single point of failure (SPOF): one server, so when it dies every notification stops. Hard to scale: the database, the cache and every processing step share one machine, so you can't grow one part without growing all of them. Performance bottleneck: building notifications is heavy work (rendering HTML emails, waiting for slow providers to answer), and doing it all in one place overloads the box at peak hours.[1]

How big is big?

Back-of-the-envelope on the book's numbers

Before you redesign anything, size the problem. In the book, the interviewer and candidate agree on this scope:[1]

📊 Volume per day10 million mobile pushes, 1 million SMS, 5 million emails
⏱️ Soft real-timeSend as soon as possible. A small delay under heavy load is fine.
📱 DevicesiOS, Android, and laptop or desktop
🔔 TriggersClient apps, or jobs scheduled on the server side
🙅 Opt-outUsers can turn notifications off and stop getting them

Interviewers like a quick per-second estimate (that's step 1 of the book's framework[2]). Divide each daily number by the seconds in a day. Round answers are fine: anything within 25% counts.

Napkin trick. A day has 86,400 seconds. For quick maths, call it 100,000 (105). Example: 3,000,000 a day ÷ 100,000 ≈ 30 a second. Rounding down a bit is fine; the exact answer is about 16% higher.

Three fixes

Build the improved design

The book fixes version 1 with three moves:[1]

  1. Move the database and cache out of the notification server, so each can scale on its own.
  2. Add more notification servers and let them scale out automatically (horizontal scaling: more machines, not a bigger machine).
  3. Add message queues to decouple the parts. A queue is a waiting line: one side drops messages in, the other side takes them out at its own speed, and neither has to wait for the other.

Why queues, in the book's words (paraphrased). Queues remove the dependency between components, and they act as buffers when a large batch of notifications must go out. Each notification type gets its own queue, so an outage at one third-party service doesn't hold up the others.[1] Toy model: 7 workers, 30-second ticks, and an SMS send that hangs for 90 seconds before it's put back on the queue to retry.

    🎓 Level-up note (for later)

    • The queues can become the new single point of failure. The book doesn't say this. A queue that lives on one broker machine is just a new single box. Real systems replicate it: Kafka copies each partition to several brokers,[7] and RabbitMQ has quorum queues replicated with the Raft protocol.[6]
    • Split by priority, not just channel. The book splits queues by channel only. Real systems often add priority lanes too. A one-time login code (OTP) should never wait behind a 2-million-email marketing blast, even though both are "email".
    • The sample path is a typo. The book's email example posts to /v/sms/send. Version and channel your paths clearly, e.g. /v1/email/send.
    • Stateless servers are what make autoscaling easy. The notification servers keep no user data of their own (it's all in the cache and DB), so any server can answer any request, and adding or removing one is safe.
