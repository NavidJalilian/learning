Quest 2.1 · Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

The Byte Ladder

Every storage estimate in a system design interview uses five units and one trick. Climb from kilobytes to petabytes, then multiply hundreds of millions of anything in your head.

🎯

Your win today: you can say "2 to the 30 is about a billion, so a gigabyte", work out "300 million users × 1 KB" in your head (300 GB), and you never write a number on the whiteboard without its unit.

1

Climb the byte ladder

Five rungs that cover every estimate you'll ever do

Chapter 2 opens with an idea from Jeff Dean, a Senior Fellow at Google. A back-of-the-envelope estimate is a rough number you build from a thought experiment plus a few well-known performance figures. You use it to get a feel for which designs could meet your requirements before you build anything.[1][2] In an interview, the point is to show you can reason about scale. Nobody expects precision.

Data sizes get huge, but the maths stays simple if you know the units. Computers count in powers of two, and the book's Table 2-1 gives five that matter. Each one is roughly a round power of ten.[1]

Build the ladder. Tap a chip, then tap the rung it belongs on. Each rung needs a word (how many bytes, roughly) and a unit. Wrong guesses just bounce back. This part is practice.

The 2.4% white lie

KB or KiB? Strictly, KB, MB, GB are decimal (103, 106, 109). The binary versions have their own names: KiB, MiB, GiB (kibibyte, mebibyte, gibibyte) mean exactly 210, 220, 230.[4] The book, like most engineers in conversation, blurs the two. In an interview, just say "I'll treat 210 as 1,000" and move on.
3

Count the zeros

Big multiplications without a calculator

Here is the one trick. Write every number as a small number × 10something (that's scientific notation; the small number is called the mantissa). Then:

  1. Multiply the small numbers. 3 × 1 = 3.
  2. Add the exponents. 108 × 103 = 1011.
  3. Map the exponent to a unit with the ladder: 3 → K, 6 → M, 9 → G, 12 → T, 15 → P. 1011 is 100 × 109, so 3 × 1011 bytes = 300 GB.

Percentages are just small numbers too: 10% is × 0.1, which is 10−1. Four cards. Tap a factor chip to see it in powers of ten, then type your answer and pick a unit. Anything within 10% counts.

4

Round like a pro

The book's tips: approximate, and label every unit

The chapter ends with tips. Two of them are about arithmetic hygiene.[1]

Tip 1: round and approximate. Working out 99,987 ÷ 9.1 by hand burns interview minutes for no gain. Round it to 100,000 ÷ 10 and you have 10,000 in a second. Precision isn't expected.

Tip 2: label your units. If you write "5" on the board, is it 5 KB or 5 MB? Twenty minutes later you won't remember either, and one slipped unit is a 1,000× error. Write 5 MB.

Handy constant (not from the book, but standard). A day has 86,400 seconds, which is about 105. So 1 million events a day is about 10 per second (exactly 11.6), and 1 billion a day is about 10,000 per second (exactly 11,574). It runs about 14% low, which is why the book's Twitter example gets ~3,500 QPS where this shortcut gives ~3,000. Quest 2.4 does that maths properly.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

  • The drift is real. 210 ≈ 1,000 is off by 2.4% per rung, about 10% at TB and 12.6% at PB. Fine for estimates. Not fine for billing, capacity contracts or "will this exactly fit in RAM" questions.
  • KB vs KiB. Standards bodies say KB/MB/GB are decimal and KiB/MiB/GiB are binary. Windows shows binary sizes labelled GB, while macOS (since 2009) shows decimal ones, so the same drive looks different on each, which is why drive sizes look "wrong".[4]
  • 1 character = 1 byte only holds for plain English text. Real text is usually UTF-8, where accented and non-Latin characters take 2–3 bytes and emoji take 4. A chat app in Japanese stores about 3× the bytes per character.
  • 86,400 ≈ 105 is a common shortcut, not the book's. It makes per-second rates about 14% too low. Say so if you use it.
