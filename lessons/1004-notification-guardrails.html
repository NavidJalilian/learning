1

Templates

Don't build every message from scratch

A big notification system sends millions of notifications a day, and most of them look alike: "your order shipped", "it's back in stock", "here is your login code". So the book doesn't have every service write its own text. It uses a notification template: a ready-made message with blanks, called parameters, that you fill in for each send. The styling and tracking links are baked into the template too.[1]

The book's example is a seasonal "it's back, for a short time" promo with two blanks, [ITEM NAME] and [DATE], plus a CTA (call to action: the button you want people to press). Here's one like it. Fill the blanks and the same template renders for two channels at once.

2

The opt-in gate

Notification settings

People get a lot of notifications every day and feel swamped quickly. So apps let users say, in detail, what they want. The book stores this in a notification setting table with three columns:[1]

  • user_id (bigint): who.
  • channel (varchar): push, email or SMS.
  • opt_in (boolean): whether they agreed to get notifications on that channel. Opt in = said yes. Opt out = said no.

The rule: before any notification is sent, check that the user has opted in to that channel. In the flow from 10.2, this lookup happens when the notification server fetches the user's info, device token and settings from the cache or database, before anything goes on a queue.

Did the password reset trip you up? Under the book's table it has to be blocked: Ana opted out of email, full stop. But she just asked for that email. The book's three columns can't tell "marketing" from "you asked for this". Flip on real-world rules to see how products handle it.
Beyond the book. Real products store settings per category × channel (marketing, social, account…), not just per channel. Transactional messages, the ones a user triggered or needs (password resets, login codes, receipts, delivery updates), usually go out even if marketing is switched off. And there are laws: in the US, CAN-SPAM says every commercial email needs a working way to opt out, and opt-outs must be honoured within 10 business days.[5] In the EU, rules like GDPR generally require consent before marketing messages.
3

Frequency cap

Rate limiting per user

Ana opted in to push. That doesn't mean she wants 14 pushes a day. The book adds rate limiting here: cap how many notifications one user can receive. The reason is blunt: send too often and people turn notifications off completely, and then you've lost the channel for everything, including the messages that matter.[1]

You already know how to build one from Chapter 4: a token bucket or sliding window, keyed by user_id (and channel) instead of by IP address.[2]

Where does it sit? In the book's final design, rate limiting lives on the notification servers, next to authentication. Over-limit messages are dropped before they're queued, so no worker or provider wastes effort on them.[1]
Beyond the book. Critical and transactional messages (login alerts, one-time codes) are normally exempt from marketing caps, as you just found. And there's a second, separate limit: the providers rate-limit you. SMS gateways and push services throttle senders who push too hard, so workers need back-off too.

Lock the door, count the clicks

appKey/appSecret + events tracking

Security. For iOS and Android apps, the book secures the push notification APIs with an appKey and an appSecret. The key says who you are. The secret proves it, like a username and password for a program. Only authenticated or verified clients get to send pushes.[1] Back in 10.2 the book also said the send API should only be open to internal services or verified clients, to stop spam.

Honest caveats. (1) appKey/appSecret is the book's general framing. The real providers differ: APNs authenticates your server with a JSON Web Token signed by a .p8 key, or with a TLS certificate,[3] and FCM's HTTP v1 API takes short-lived OAuth 2 tokens made from a service account.[4] Internal callers often use service-to-service auth such as mutual TLS. (2) "Delivered" and "opened" are softer than they look. A success from APNs means "accepted", not "on the screen", and FCM gives delivery data as reports, so many apps report "received" back themselves. Email "opens" come from a tiny tracking image, and Apple Mail Privacy Protection loads those images automatically, so open rates get inflated.

Read it top to bottom. At the door, the notification servers authenticate the caller, rate-limit per user, check opt-in, and build the text from a template. Bad traffic dies here, before it costs anything. After the queue, workers write to the notification log so nothing is lost, put failed sends back on the queue to retry a set number of times, and everything reports events to analytics. The book also adds monitoring: watch how many notifications are waiting in the queues, and add workers when the number climbs.[1]

🎓 Level-up note

Four things the book's version simplifies. Settings are per channel in the book; real products split them by category too, and transactional messages usually ignore marketing opt-outs. Auth: "appKey/appSecret" is a provider-style idea. APNs uses a .p8-signed JWT or a certificate, FCM uses OAuth 2 service-account tokens, and internal services usually use mutual TLS or signed tokens. Metrics: "delivered" and "opened" are fuzzy. Push providers don't promise per-device receipts, and email open pixels are skewed by privacy features. Law: commercial email and SMS carry legal duties the book skips: an unsubscribe link that works (CAN-SPAM), and consent rules for texts and for EU users.
