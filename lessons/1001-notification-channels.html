Quest 10.1 · Design a Notification System

Three Roads to the Lock Screen

Your app needs to tap a user on the shoulder. There are three ways to do it, and on every one of them a company you don't own carries the message the last mile. Map the roads, build a real push request, and find out what you must collect before you can send anything.

🎯

Your win today: you can explain how one notification reaches an iPhone, an Android phone, a phone number and an inbox, name the third-party service on each road, and say what data you must have collected first (device token, phone number, email).

1

Three roads, one ping

Push, SMS, email, and who carries each

A notification tells a user something they care about: breaking news, a product update, an event, an offer. The book covers three kinds:[1]

  • Mobile push: the banner that slides onto a phone's screen, sent to an app installed on that phone.
  • SMS: a plain text message to a phone number.
  • Email: a message to an inbox.

Here's the twist. Your servers never deliver any of these themselves. In the book your system is called the provider: it builds the message and hands it to a third-party delivery service (a company that runs the last mile for you). Every road has the same shape: Provider → delivery service → user's device.

Your move: tap a service in the tray, then tap the road it belongs on. One chip isn't a real road at all.

Delivery services (tap one, then tap a road)
Pick a chip to start.
Every road, same shape. iOS push goes through APNs (Apple Push Notification service). Android push goes through FCM (Firebase Cloud Messaging, Google's service). SMS goes through services like Twilio or Nexmo (now called Vonage). Email goes through services like SendGrid or Mailchimp.[1] You could run your own mail servers, but the book says most companies buy a commercial service because it gets more mail delivered and comes with analytics. Fewer of your emails land in spam, and you can see what was opened.
The book's scale, for later quests. The interview in this chapter asks for about 10 million push notifications, 1 million SMS and 5 million emails a day. It should be "soft real-time": delivered fast, but a small delay under heavy load is fine.[1]

Anatomy of an iOS push

Build the request APNs needs

Let's follow the iOS road closely. Your provider builds a notification request and sends it to APNs, Apple's service that carries pushes to iPhones and iPads.[2] The book says the request needs exactly two things:[1]

  • A device token: a unique ID that APNs gives to one install of your app on one device. It's the "address" of that phone. You must have saved it earlier.
  • A payload: a small JSON object (text in {"key": value} form) that says what to show: a title, a body, a badge number.

Your move: pick the two right items from the tray to fill the envelope. Then edit the message and send it.

Beyond the book: tokens go stale. Tokens die when the app is uninstalled, and they can also change. When that happens, APNs answers with status 410 and reason Unregistered (or 400 BadDeviceToken for a token that was never valid).[4] Don't retry those. Delete the token: retrying a dead one just wastes work. FCM's version of this error is called UNREGISTERED.[6]

Android, and when a road is closed
FCM, and keeping providers pluggable

    

      
Android follows the same shape: Provider → FCM → Android device. FCM (Firebase Cloud Messaging) is Google's push service, and like APNs it needs a token for the device plus a payload.[5]

      
The book makes one big design point here: extensibility. A good notification system makes it easy to plug in or unplug a third-party service, because a service may not work in a new market, or may not be around in the future. Its example: FCM is not available in China, so apps there use other push services such as JPush or Pushy.[1]

      
Your move: send a ping in a working market. Then launch in China and fix the road.

That's the point. Hide each provider behind one small "send" interface per channel. Then a new market is a config change (android.provider[CN] = "JPush"), not a hunt through every feature. In the book's improved design (next quest), each channel gets its own workers, and only they talk to the third-party service, so the swap stays in one place. Why FCM fails there: most Android phones sold in mainland China ship without Google Play services, and FCM depends on them.

Collect the addresses first
Contact info gathering flow, then spot the bug

    

      
You can't send anything without an address: a device token for push, a phone number for SMS, an email for email. The book's flow: when a user installs the app or signs up for the first time, the API servers collect their contact info and store it in the database. The request passes through a load balancer on the way (a box that spreads requests across many API servers).[1]

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

      
    
        
  • The book's payload is simplified. A real APNs request goes over HTTP/2, and it also carries headers such as apns-topic (your app's ID), apns-push-type and apns-priority. The JSON body has a 4 KB limit for normal pushes.[3]
    • 
        
  • Prune tokens on purpose. Delete a token when APNs says 410 Unregistered or FCM says UNREGISTERED. Firebase also recommends treating tokens that haven't been refreshed in a long while as stale and dropping them. A last_logged_in_at-style timestamp is what makes that possible.[6]
    • 
        
  • Phone numbers are strings. Store them in E.164 form: a +, the country code, then the number, at most 15 digits in all (for example +447911123456).[10] The book's integer column is a sketch, not advice.
    • 
        
  • Names change; roads don't. Nexmo is now Vonage. In China, many Android phones also come with their maker's own push service. The book's provider names are examples, not endorsements. What matters in the interview is "a third-party service per channel, behind an interface".
