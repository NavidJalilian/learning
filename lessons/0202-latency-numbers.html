Quest 2.2 · Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

Nanoseconds to Netherlands

Some things your computer does take half a billionth of a second. Others take a sixth of a second. Learn the ladder between them, feel it at human scale, and use it to win design arguments.

🎯

Your win today: you can rank memory, disk, data-center and cross-ocean costs from memory, quote the rough numbers (100 ns, 10 ms, 0.5 ms, 150 ms), and use them to justify a cache, batching, or compression.

Order the universe

Sort 13 operations from fastest to slowest

Every design choice is secretly a bet on how long something takes. Around 2009, Google engineer Jeff Dean presented a list of rough timings that programmers still quote. The book reprints it as Table 2-2.[1][3] First, the three units:

nsnanosecond = 10−9 s, one billionth of a second
µsmicrosecond = 10−6 s = 1,000 ns
msmillisecond = 10−3 s = 1,000 µs
Jargon decoder
  • L1 / L2 cache: tiny, very fast memory built into the CPU chip. L1 is the smallest and fastest.
  • Branch mispredict: the CPU guessed the wrong side of an if ahead of time and has to throw that work away.
  • Mutex lock/unlock: grabbing and releasing a lock so only one thread touches some data at a time.
  • Main memory: RAM, outside the CPU chip. Zippy: Google's fast compressor, open-sourced as Snappy.[6]
  • Disk seek: a spinning hard drive moving its read head to the right spot. Round trip: a message goes there and the reply comes back.

    If an L1 hit took one second

    Stretch time until your gut understands it

    Nobody can feel a nanosecond. So cheat: multiply every number in the table by 2 billion. Now an L1 cache hit (0.5 ns) takes exactly 1 second, and everything else stretches by the same amount. The ratios stay true. Only the units change.

      Old numbers, new hardware

      What has changed since 2010, and what never will

      The table describes hardware from around 2010. Some rows have aged a lot. Others can't move at all. The book's reference for these numbers, Colin Scott's interactive page, shows how each row changes by year.[2]

      Book values, around 2010

      "Today" values are rough ranges for a typical modern server, not a benchmark. The faint line marks where each bar sat in 2010.

      What changed. SSDs (solid-state drives: flash memory, no spinning platter, no moving head) replaced spinning disks for hot data. A random SSD read takes tens of microseconds instead of a 10 ms seek, and fast NVMe SSDs read gigabytes per second. Networks inside data centers went from 1 Gbps to 10–100 Gbps. CPU caches and RAM latency barely moved.

      What will never change: the speed of light. Press the button to send a pulse through fiber from California to the Netherlands and back, at the best speed physics allows.

      9,000 km of fiber, one way
      light in fiber ≈ 200,000 km/s
      Physics sets the floor. Light in glass fiber travels at about two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum. California to the Netherlands is roughly 9,000 km in a straight line along the globe, so even a perfect cable needs about 90 ms for the round trip. Real cables take longer routes and pass through routers, which is how you get the table's ~150 ms. No hardware upgrade fixes that. Only moving the data closer does.

      🎓 Level-up note (for later)

      Treat every number as an order of magnitude. Versions of the list disagree a little. Jeff Dean's 2009 slides give mutex lock/unlock as 25 ns, Zippy as 3 µs per KB and a 1 MB disk read as 20 ms.[3] The book uses 100 ns, 10 µs and 30 ms. Popular copies such as Jonas Bonér's gist add SSD rows and credit Dean and Peter Norvig, whose 2001 essay had an earlier timing table.[4][5] Colin Scott's chart fills in later years by extrapolating trends, so it isn't a measured benchmark either.[2] In an interview, quote the book's numbers, then add: "SSDs shrank the disk gap, but memory is still far faster, and cross-region latency is physics."
