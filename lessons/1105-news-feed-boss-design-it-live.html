Deep dive: the fanout call

Step 3 · the decision the interviewer is waiting for

The book's deep dive covers two halves: publishing (how one post reaches many feeds) and retrieval (how a list of IDs becomes a feed you can scroll).[1] Three quick rounds. Mistakes cost hearts.

Honest caveat: real feeds. Twitter's 2012 "Timelines at Scale" talk describes something close to the book: tweet IDs pushed into capped, in-memory timelines, and real pain fanning out accounts with tens of millions of followers.[3] Facebook's production feed (Multifeed) goes the other way: it pulls recent activity from in-memory "leaf" servers and ranks it at read time.[4] And Facebook's "graph DB" is TAO, a graph-shaped cache over sharded MySQL.[5]

Boss: the curveball barrage

Step 3 · "what happens when…?"

Now the interviewer stops nodding and starts poking holes. Eight curveballs, mixed from the whole chapter. A right answer costs about a minute; a wrong one costs a heart and some backtracking.

Honest caveats from the curveballs. (1) Message queues normally promise at-least-once delivery: a job can arrive twice. Exactly-once end to end is rarely worth the cost here, so the usual answer is to make the writes idempotent (safe to repeat). (2) Rebuilding a lost feed, or one for a returning user, is a pull. In practice push and pull aren't either/or: push is the fast path and pull is the fallback.

    🎓 Level-up note

    The book's design is a clean interview answer. Real feeds add ranking (first gather candidate posts, then score them with ML models; Twitter open-sourced its pipeline[7]), ads, de-duplication and cursor-based paging. And "push vs pull" doesn't have to be a per-account switch: the Feeding Frenzy paper decides it for each pair of users, comparing how often one posts with how often the other reads.[6] Mentioning either in a wrap-up is a strong senior signal, as long as it's one sentence and not a new deep dive.
