Count the Nines

"99.9% uptime" sounds like "never down". It isn't. Turn the dial, do the maths, chain some services together, and see how much downtime each nine really allows.

1

What a nine buys you

From 99% to 99.9999%, on a calendar

High availability means a system stays up and working for a long, unbroken stretch of time. You measure it as the percentage of time the system is up. 100% would mean it is never down. Real services land somewhere between 99% and 100%.[1]

A service level agreement (SLA) is the deal between a provider and its customers that writes down how much uptime the service will deliver. Amazon, Google and Microsoft set their cloud SLAs at 99.9% or higher.[2][3][4] People count uptime in nines: 99.9% is "three nines", 99.99% is "four nines". More nines, less downtime.

Do the math yourself

Downtime = (1 − availability) × period

You don't need to memorise the table. One line rebuilds any cell of it:

allowed downtime = (1 − availability) × period

The part after "1 −" is the fraction of time you're allowed to be down. 99.9% up means 0.1% down, which is 0.001. The book's table uses a 365.25-day year, and a month that is 1/12 of that (about 30.44 days).[1] Handy period sizes:

1 year = 8,766 h = 525,960 min 1 month ≈ 730.5 h ≈ 43,830 min 1 day = 1,440 min = 86,400 s

Fill in each answer, pick a unit, and press Check. Within 10% counts, in any unit you like (52.6 min or 0.88 h are both fine). A right answer earns XP. A wrong one costs a heart and shows the working.

The "divide by 10" staircase (downtime per year)

2 nines99%3 nines99.9%4 nines99.99%5 nines99.999%6 nines99.9999%

Each extra nine makes the "down" fraction 10 times smaller: 0.01 → 0.001 → 0.0001… So each step is a tenth of the one before. Odd targets sit in between. 99.95% allows half of 99.9%'s downtime: 0.0005 instead of 0.001.

3

Chains and spares

Dependencies multiply; redundancy adds nines

This part isn't in the book chapter, but interviewers love to ask it.[6] Two rules cover almost every case:

  • In a row (series): if a request needs A and B and C to all work, multiply: A × B × C. Every extra hard dependency (something you can't work without) pulls the total down.
  • Side by side (parallel): if any one of two copies can serve the request, you're only down when both are down at once: 1 − (1 − A)². Spares (also called redundancy) push the total up.

Read the fine print

SLA vs SLO vs SLI, and what an SLA really promises

The book defines only the SLA. Google's SRE book adds two more terms you'll hear in senior conversations:[5]

  • SLI (service level indicator): a number you measure. For example, "the share of requests that succeeded over the last 30 days".
  • SLO (service level objective): your own target for that number. For example, "99.9% of requests succeed".
  • SLA (service level agreement): the contract with customers, with a consequence if you miss it. Cloud SLAs usually pay service credits (a partial refund on your bill). They don't promise the service will actually be up.[2]

Teams set the SLO tighter than the SLA, so they notice trouble before it costs money. Tap the right bin for each card.

The error budget

Error budget = 1 − SLO. At a 99.9% SLO, about 43.8 minutes a month may fail. That's a budget you can spend on risky things like deploys and experiments. When it runs out, the SRE book's rule is to halt risky launches until it recovers.[6] Ship some releases and watch it drain.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

  • 365 vs 365.25 days. Table 2-3 uses a 365.25-day year. With 365 days the yearly numbers shift by under 0.1% (8.76 h instead of 8.77 h). That doesn't matter in an interview.
  • Not in the book. The series and parallel maths, SLO, SLI and error budgets come from Google's SRE book. They are common senior-level follow-ups.[5]
  • Independence is the big lie. Redundancy maths assumes failures are independent. Real outages are often correlated: a shared region, power feed, config push or bad deploy takes out every copy at once. Real availability ends up below what the formula says.
  • Time-based vs request-based. The nines table counts minutes down. Many teams measure requests instead ("99.9% of requests succeed"), because a 10-minute blip at 3 a.m. hurts fewer users than one at noon.[6]
  • An SLA is a refund policy. A provider SLA is a contract with service credits. It is not a promise that the service will be up. The exact percentages differ by product and change over time.
