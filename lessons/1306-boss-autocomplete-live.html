Step 3 · deep dive (10–25 min)

The table is gone. In its place: a trie (a prefix tree: each node is one more character, so all queries starting with "tw" sit under the same branch). Two paths feed and read it. Tap the 11 components in the order data flows along their path. The write path (amber) builds the trie; the read path (teal) serves it. Out-of-order taps cost a minute.

Honest caveat: own the staleness. The trie is rebuilt weekly, and the browser may keep a reply for an hour (max-age=3600). So a suggestion can be up to a week, plus an hour, out of date. That's fine for the book's requirements. Say it out loud rather than hoping nobody asks. The Trie DB also has two storage options in the book: a document store holding weekly snapshots of the whole trie, or a key-value store where each prefix is a key and its node's data is the value.

Boss: the curveball barrage

Step 3 · "what happens when…?"

Now Maya stops nodding and starts poking holes. Nine curveballs, drawn from the whole chapter. Each right answer takes 1.5 game-minutes. A wrong one costs a heart and an extra minute of backtracking.

Honest caveats from the curveballs. (1) "Warm the new cache before switching" goes beyond the book, which only says a miss refills the cache. It's standard practice, and a good thing to offer. (2) max-age=3600 is the example the book gives from Google's own reply headers at the time of writing; real values vary and change. Cache-Control is a standard HTTP header[5]. (3) Splitting a hot letter on its second character is the book's "shard at the second level" idea; the shard map manager decides where each range lives.

Wrap it up

Step 4 · the last 3–5 minutes

The book's rule: don't stop once the design is drawn[2]. The autocomplete chapter ends with three follow-ups an interviewer is likely to ask: other languages, other countries, and trending queries. A strong close brings them up before you're asked, and names the design's weak spots.

Pick exactly 3 closing moves, then deliver them.

Honest caveat: popularity is the only ranking signal. The book ranks suggestions by how often they were searched, nothing else. Real systems also weigh who you are (personalization), how recent a query is (freshness) and where you are (location). Mentioning those as future signals is a strong close. Don't build them unless the interviewer asks. For trending queries, the book points to stream processing tools such as Apache Kafka[6], Spark Streaming[7] and Storm[8].

🎓 Level-up note

Clicking the right answer is not the same as saying it. Run this interview out loud once this week: set a 45-minute timer, draw both paths on paper, and talk through every curveball from stage 4 without looking. Three deeper points to have ready. (1) Caching the top 5 on every node trades a lot of memory for speed; say so. (2) A key-value Trie DB (prefix → node data) is basically the trie flattened into a lookup table, which is why it shards cleanly by prefix. (3) The weekly rebuild is the real reason trending queries are hard: a news event is over before the next rebuild. That's why the book's answer for trends is a different, streaming system rather than a faster weekly job.
