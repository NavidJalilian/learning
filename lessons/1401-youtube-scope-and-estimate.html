Napkin maths: storage

Calculate, then check

Back-of-the-envelope estimation means rough maths to find the order of magnitude (is it gigabytes or petabytes?) and the cost that dominates. It is not an exact figure. The book's assumptions:[1]

5 million DAUeach watches 5 videos/day10% upload 1 video/dayaverage video = 300 MB

DAU = daily active users, the number of different people who use the product on a given day.

✓ 150 TB a day, the book's number. That's about 55 PB a year (150 TB × 365), and it counts only the original uploads. Real systems store more. Two things the book leaves out: transcoded versions (the same video re-encoded at 360p, 720p, 1080p and so on, so every device can play it) and replicas (extra copies on other machines, so a dead disk loses nothing).

This sim treats each extra version as the same size as the original. Real lower-resolution versions are smaller, so it overshoots a little, but the lesson holds: the factors multiply.

4

Napkin maths: the CDN bill

Find the cost that dominates

A CDN (content delivery network) is a fleet of servers spread around the world. Each one caches popular files and serves them from close to the viewer, so a video doesn't cross an ocean every time someone presses play. Cloud CDNs charge for egress: every gigabyte they send out to viewers.

The book prices it with Amazon CloudFront at $0.02 per GB, assumes all traffic is served in the US, and counts only video streaming.[1][2] Same assumptions as before: 5 million DAU, 5 videos each a day, 300 MB a video.

That's the book's whole point. Even at the cheapest published price, serving bytes is the bill that dominates: about $55 million a year. That's why the deep dive (Quest 14.5) spends a whole section on cost: serve only popular videos from the CDN, serve the long tail from your own servers, and more.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

  • 5 million DAU is tiny next to real YouTube (2 billion people a month). It's an interview simplification so the maths fits on a napkin. The method is what matters.
  • 150 TB a day is originals only. Multiply by the number of transcoded versions (often 5 or more) and by replication (often 3 copies), and the real figure is many times larger.
  • $0.02 per GB is CloudFront's cheapest published US tier, which only kicks in above several petabytes a month. This design sends about 7.5 PB a day, so nearly all of it would be in that tier. A small site pays the first-tier price, about $0.085, and very large customers negotiate private rates.[2]
  • Storage isn't free either. A year of originals (~55 PB) at a typical cloud object-storage list price of roughly $0.02 per GB-month comes to around a million dollars a month. That's real money, but still several times less than the CDN's ~$4.5 million a month.
  • The CDN estimate is pessimistic in one way (it assumes every view streams the whole 300 MB file) and optimistic in another (US prices are among the cheapest, and the users are largely international).
