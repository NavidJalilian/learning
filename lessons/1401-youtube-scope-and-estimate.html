Size Up the Tube

Guess the giant

How big is YouTube, really?

The book opens the chapter with a handful of YouTube numbers from around 2019–2020.[1] Before you see them, guess. Drag the slider, then tap Lock. Most sliders jump by powers of ten, because at this size the order of magnitude (millions? billions?) is what matters.

Within 2× of the real figure (or 10 percentage points for a %) earns XP. A wild miss costs nothing. Guessing is the point.

Honest caveat. These figures are around 2019–2020 and come from third-party stat roundups that the book quotes. Treat them as "order of magnitude", not as today's facts. The lesson they teach still holds: YouTube is far too big to design whole in one interview.

Napkin maths: storage

Calculate, then check

Back-of-the-envelope estimation means rough maths done fast. You're not after an exact figure. You want the order of magnitude and which cost is biggest. The book's assumptions:[1]

5Mdaily active users (DAU)
5videos watched per user per day
10%of users upload 1 video a day
300 MBaverage video size
55 PB
stored after 1 year
  • ✓Now make it realistic: set copies to 3, the usual replication factor (each file stored on 3 machines so one dead disk loses nothing)
Honest caveat. The book's 150 TB/day counts the original uploads only. Real storage multiplies by the number of transcoded versions (different resolutions, often 5 or more) and by replication (often 3 copies). Lower-resolution versions are smaller than the original, so "×5" is a ceiling, not an exact figure. Either way, the real bill is many times the napkin number.
4

Napkin maths: the CDN bill

Find the cost that dominates

A CDN (content delivery network) is a fleet of servers spread around the world. It keeps copies of your files and serves each viewer from a server near them, so video starts fast. You pay the CDN for every gigabyte it sends out. That outgoing traffic is called egress.

The book prices it with Amazon CloudFront at $0.02 per GB in the US.[1][2] Same assumptions as before: 5M DAU, 5 videos each a day, 300 MB per video. Every view streams from the CDN.

  • ✓Play with the knobs: get the bill under $50,000 a day. Which knob moves it most?
Honest caveat. $0.02/GB is CloudFront's cheapest published US tier. You only reach it after petabytes of traffic in a month. The first tier lists at several times that, and very large customers negotiate private rates.[2] The estimate also assumes every view streams the whole 300 MB file. In real life many viewers stop early or watch at a lower quality. Prices change, so quote the method, not the cents.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

  • Old stats. 2B MAU, 5B views a day and $15.1B ad revenue are 2019–2020 figures from third-party roundups. Use them for scale, not as facts to quote.
  • Tiny DAU. 5M DAU is an interview simplification. The method (users × rate × size) is what transfers.
  • Originals only. 150 TB/day ignores transcoded versions and replicas. A realistic figure is several times larger, and it adds up every day you keep the videos.
  • The cheapest price tier. $0.02/GB is CloudFront's lowest published US tier, reached only at huge monthly volume. Smaller customers pay several times that, big ones negotiate. The estimate also assumes every view downloads the full 300 MB.
  • "Blob storage" is cloud storage for big binary files (a BLOB is a binary large object: video, images, backups).[3] You'll meet it again in 14.2.
