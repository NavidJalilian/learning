Ask Before You Build

The interviewer says six words: "Design a news feed." The candidates who do well don't start drawing. They start asking. Learn which questions matter, what to do when the answer is "you decide", and how to pin the scope down.

🎯

Your win today: in the first 3–10 minutes, ask the questions that matter, write down your assumptions, and lock the scope of "design a news feed" with the book's numbers.

1

Don't Sprint to the Answer

Why jumping straight in backfires

The book splits a 45-minute interview into four steps. Step 1 is "understand the problem and establish design scope", and it gets about 3 to 10 minutes.[1] Scope means the edges of the problem: what's in, what's out, and how big it has to be.

Engineers love hard problems, so the urge is to jump straight to a final design. The book warns against it. A fast answer earns no bonus points. Answering before you understand the requirements is a big red flag, because there's no single right answer to look up. The interview isn't a trivia quiz.[1] Its advice: slow down, think, and ask.

Hidden requirements are the trap. 2

The Question Deck

Four question families that matter

The book suggests questions from four families:[1]

  • Features: what exactly are we building?
  • Users: how many people use the product? A common measure is DAU, daily active users: how many different people use it on a given day.
  • Growth: how fast will it grow? What scale do we expect in 3 months, 6 months, a year?
  • Stack: what technology does the company already use, and which existing services could we reuse to keep the design simple?

A good question changes the design. A poor one burns a minute and tells the interviewer you can't tell the difference. Your budget is 6 minutes, and each question costs 1. Light all four family badges before time runs out.

3

Answer or Assume

When the interviewer says "you decide"

When you ask a question, the book says one of two things happens. The interviewer answers it, or tells you to make an assumption. Either way, write it on the whiteboard, so both of you can see it and you can point back to it later.[1]

An assumption is a decision you make yourself when nobody gives you the answer. Say it out loud and it's a decision you own. Keep it in your head and it's a hidden risk: the interviewer may be picturing something else. Good assumptions keep things simple, stay realistic, and come with a one-line reason.

This interviewer likes to say "you decide". Ask all four questions, handle each reply, then fast-forward to minute 25 and see what your choices cost.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

Name both lists. Many interviewers now expect step 1 to end with two explicit lists: functional requirements (what the system does: post, view feed) and non-functional requirements (how well it does it: latency, availability, consistency). The book covers this implicitly. Saying both lists out loud is a cheap win.

When do the numbers come? Some interviewers want back-of-the-envelope estimates in step 1, some in step 2, some never. The book places them in step 2 as a sanity check on the design.[3] Ask which they prefer. The reads-per-second calculation in stage 4 is our extension, not part of Chapter 3.

The numbers are illustrative. 5,000 friends and 10 million DAU are the book's example values for this exercise, not real Facebook figures. In an interview, the numbers are whatever you and the interviewer agree on, which is exactly why you write them down.
