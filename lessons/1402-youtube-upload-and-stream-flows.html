Upload Lane, Watch Lane

1

Three boxes

Who serves what?

In Quest 14.1 you scoped the job: people upload videos and people watch them, on phones, laptops and smart TVs, with 5 million users a day. Now it's step 2 of the interview: sketch a high-level design and get the interviewer to agree with it.

The book starts with just three boxes:[1]

  • Client: the app on a computer, a phone or a smart TV.
  • CDN (content delivery network: servers spread around the world that keep copies of files close to users). The videos are stored here. When you press Play, the video streams from the CDN.
  • API servers: everything else. Feed recommendations, creating the upload URL, updating the metadata database and cache, user signup, and so on.

Your turn. Requests arrive from the client one at a time. Send each one to the box that serves it.

      Don't build everything. The book's advice for this chapter: storing and shipping huge files is a solved problem, so reuse existing cloud services. Use a ready-made CDN, and use blob storage for the files. "Blob" means binary large object: a big chunk of bytes with no structure the store cares about, like a video.[2] A blob store such as Amazon S3 keeps those files cheaply and safely. Then spend your interview minutes on the parts that are really yours: the flows you'll trace in stages 2 and 3.[1]

      Stream, don't download

      How the video reaches the screen

      Downloading means copying the whole file to your device before it plays. Streaming means your device keeps receiving small pieces of the video and plays each one as it arrives, so playback starts almost at once.[1]

      Two phones open the same 300 MB clip (about 10 minutes of video, the book's average upload size) on a 20 Mbit/s connection. The stream phone fetches it in 4-second pieces. Predict first: how long until each one shows its first frame?

      The maths. 300 MB is 2,400 megabits, and 2,400 ÷ 20 Mbit/s = 120 seconds before the download phone can start. A 4-second piece at 4 Mbit/s is only 16 megabits, so it arrives in 0.8 s (plus a few network round trips). The stream phone also stops fetching when it is about 30 seconds ahead. Close the app after a minute and it has used about 45 MB, not 300.

      Where do those pieces come from? In the book's design, straight from the CDN. The CDN server nearest to you (its edge) delivers the video, so the delay is tiny.[1] Tap a city to compare its nearest edge with a single origin server in Virginia, USA.

      A streaming protocol is an agreed way to control how the pieces of a video are sent. The book lists four. Match each name to what it stands for.

      What you need to know. Not the internals. You need to know that different protocols support different video encodings and different players, so you pick the one that fits the devices you must serve.[1] Honest caveat: today HLS (written up as RFC 8216)[3] and MPEG-DASH do most of the work. Smooth Streaming and HDS are mostly legacy. All four are adaptive: the player can switch quality as your connection changes.[4] Quest 14.3 shows how that works.

      🎓 Level-up note (for later)

      • Duplicates. Message queues usually deliver at least once, so the completion handler may see the same "done" event twice. Its DB and cache writes should be idempotent: safe to apply twice with the same result (for example "set status = ready", not "add 1 to the count").
      • Not all at once. The book treats "transcoding complete" as one event. Real YouTube publishes lower resolutions first and adds HD and 4K later, because the big versions take longer to process. That turns one completion event into several.
      • The upload URL. The API servers "generate the upload URL" so the heavy bytes can skip them and go straight to blob storage. Quest 14.5 makes that safe with pre-signed URLs.
