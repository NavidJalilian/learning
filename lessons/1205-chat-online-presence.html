Quest 12.5 · Design a Chat System

The Green Dot

Every chat app shows a little green dot next to friends who are online. Behind that dot sit a database write, a steady heartbeat, and a fan-out that turns scary at 100,000 members.

🎯

Your win today: you can explain how presence servers track online status, why marking a user offline on every disconnect is bad, how a heartbeat with a timeout fixes it, and how status changes fan out to friends, plus why that breaks for huge groups.

1

Login and logout

Who flips the dot

In the book's design, a group of presence servers owns the green dot. They keep track of who is online, and they talk to each phone over a WebSocket (a connection that stays open, so either side can send at any time).[1] They are stateful: each one holds live connections, which you met in Quest 12.2.

The status itself lives in a key-value store (a database that maps a key, like user:alice, to a small value). Before you press anything, predict:

Two roads, one row. Login rides the WebSocket straight to the presence servers. Logout is a normal HTTP request, so it goes through the stateless API servers first, which hand it to the presence servers. Either way, the presence servers are the only ones that write the status row.[1] Apps use a timestamp like last_active_at for "last seen 5 min ago" labels.

The tunnel problem

Why "disconnected = offline" flickers

Login and logout are easy, because the user tells you. The hard case is a disconnection: the connection just drops. The simplest rule is "connection gone, so mark them offline, and when it comes back, mark them online."

Phones lose their connection all the time. Alice is on a 2-minute train ride with three tunnels: 4, 12 and 50 seconds without signal. Bob, her friend, is watching her dot.

3

Heartbeat

Every 5 seconds: still here

The book's fix: while Alice is online, her app sends a tiny heartbeat event to the presence servers every few seconds. It just means "still here". The server keeps a timer per user. If a heartbeat arrives within x seconds, she stays online. If x seconds pass with no heartbeat, she's marked offline.[1]

The book's numbers: a heartbeat every 5 seconds, and x = 30 seconds. So a short drop just means a few missed heartbeats, and nobody notices. Same train, same tunnels. In this sim the app also sends a heartbeat the moment it reconnects.

That's the dial you're tuning. A short timeout notices real departures fast but flickers on every tunnel. A long one is calm but leaves ghosts. A shorter heartbeat interval also costs more: at 1 second it's 1,000,000 heartbeats per second. The timeout has to be several intervals long, so one or two lost heartbeats don't count as leaving. The 200,000/s figure is our worked example from the book's 1M concurrent users, not a number the book gives.
4

Fan out the dot

Pub/sub per friend pair, and the big-group trap

Alice's status changed. How do her friends find out? The book uses publish-subscribe (pub/sub): senders post to a named channel, and everyone subscribed to that channel gets the post.[3] The presence servers keep one channel per friend pair. A's change is published to channels A-B, A-C and A-D. B, C and D are subscribed, and the update reaches their phones over their WebSockets.[1]

Tap A (or the button) to flip Alice's status. Each dot on a friend shows A's status as that friend's screen shows it.

The book's fix for big groups. Don't push. Fetch online status only when a user opens the group or refreshes the friend list.[1] The trade-off: everyone else's copy of the dot can be stale until they open or refresh. For a hint like a green dot, that's fine. Slack made a similar move for big teams: its edge cache, Flannel, loads user and channel data lazily, when a client needs it, instead of sending it all up front.[2]

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

Channel per pair is a lot of channels. One channel per friend pair means millions of tiny channels. Many real systems keep one channel per user ("Alice's presence") that all her friends subscribe to, or they batch presence updates. Same idea, cheaper bookkeeping.

Heartbeats cost battery. A ping every 5 seconds keeps waking the phone's radio. Real apps often stretch the interval when the app is in the background, piggyback on other traffic, or show "last seen" instead of a live dot.

Presence is eventually consistent, on purpose. A dot can be up to 30 seconds stale. That's a fine trade for a hint. Don't reach for strong consistency here.

Pub/sub can drop messages. Redis Pub/Sub delivers at most once: a subscriber that's disconnected at that moment simply misses the post.[3] For presence that's tolerable, because the next change, a refresh or a reconnect fixes the dot.
