Step 1: Scope the Feed

Minutes 0–8 · understand the problem

The book splits every system design interview into four steps, each with a rough time budget[1]. The bar above is your game clock: every move costs minutes. The 📶 meter is your signal, meaning the evidence the interviewer writes down to decide "hire" or "no hire".

Step 1Understand the problem3–10 min
Step 2High-level design + buy-in10–15 min
Step 3Deep dive10–25 min
Step 4Wrap up3–5 min

Each turn you get four moves. A good question costs 1 minute. A time-waster costs 2 minutes and some signal. Jumping ahead costs a heart. Fill all six slots on the board; finish inside 10 minutes for a bonus.

Honest caveat. The 45-minute clock and the per-move minute costs are our game framing. The book gives a time range per step, not a scoring system. Also, in the book's version of this dialogue, the interviewer suggests newest-first ordering to keep things simple. Here you had to make the call yourself, which is just as common in real interviews.

Step 2: Sketch and Check In

Minutes 8–22 · high-level design and buy-in

The book's news feed sketch has two flows[1]. Publishing: a post is saved, then pushed into friends' feeds. Reading: the feed is assembled from friends' posts, newest first. Tap the components in the order you'd draw them. Each box costs 1 minute. A box whose parent isn't drawn yet costs an extra minute. Over-engineering costs a heart.

Step 3: The Deep Dive

Minutes 22–38 · dig where it counts

By now the interviewer has dropped a hint about what they care about. Step 3 is about spending your minutes there, avoiding rabbit holes, and thinking out loud when it gets hard. Three rounds.

Honest caveat. Chapter 3 only names "feed publishing" and "feed retrieval" as the deep-dive topics. The push/pull/hybrid details here come from the book's Chapter 11 news feed design[2] and from common practice. Twitter's home timeline is the classic real example[4]. Interviewers also vary: some drive the deep dive themselves, others let you choose. Either way, the strong move is to read their cues and check in.
4

Step 4: Wrap It Up

Minutes 38–45 · land the plane

The book's rule for the last few minutes: the interview isn't over when your diagram is done. It's over when the interviewer says so[1]. Use the time to show you see the system's weak spots and what comes next.

Pick exactly 3 closing moves, then deliver them. Strong ones take 1 minute each; weak ones take 2 and cost a heart.

Switch Seats: Grade the Candidate

You're the interviewer now · off the clock

Here is Riley's run at the same prompt. Tag each moment the way an interviewer would: ✅ Strong signal, ➖ Neutral or 🚩 Red flag. Then make the call. Watch for this: a design can be technically fine and still get a "no hire" if the process is poor. The book names over-engineering, narrow-mindedness and stubbornness as red flags[1].

To pass: at least 10 of 14 tags right and the right overall decision. Miss, and you see the answers, lose a heart and can grade again.

🎓 Level-up note

Real feeds keep going past what we drew. The "news feed cache" in production usually holds only post IDs per user, and the read path then fetches the full posts and author details from other caches (Chapter 11 calls this a multi-layer cache). Facebook's paper on scaling memcache[5] shows what that cache tier looks like at billions of reads per second: leases to stop stampedes, regional pools, and a lot of operational care. Then run this interview out loud once this week with a 45-minute timer. Wherever you stall is what to review.
