What is the news feed cache, physically? A fast in-memory store keyed by user. Just add water

Hydration, live

Step 4 gave the service Bo's feed as bare pairs of (post ID, author ID). A feed needs more than that: the author's name and profile picture, the post's text, its photos. Filling in those full objects is called hydrating the feed (like adding water to a dried-out sponge). The book's step 5 fetches them from the user cache and the post cache.[1]

Why IDs pay off here. Each post is stored once, and feeds only point at it. If Ana edits a post, every feed shows the new text on the next load. If she deletes it, the ID simply fails to hydrate and the service drops it. Nobody has to go back through thousands of feeds. Hydration is also the natural moment to re-check visibility: if someone blocked Bo after the post was fanned out, drop it here.
Real systems page. The book's GET returns "the feed". Real apps ask for one page at a time (say 20 posts) with a cursor, a marker that says "continue after this post", and hydrate only that page. Hydrating 500 posts nobody scrolls to would waste every lookup.

Media by express

Why images and videos go to a CDN

Photos and videos are huge next to a post's text, and they are read far more often than they are written. The book stores media in a CDN (content delivery network: servers spread around the world that keep copies of static files close to users) "for fast retrieval".[1] The feed's JSON carries only a URL for each image. The phone then downloads the bytes from the nearest CDN edge (one of those nearby servers), not from our web servers.[2]

Five layers of cache

The cache architecture

"The cache" is really several caches. The book splits it into five layers:[1]

  • 📰News Feed: the IDs in each user's feed.
  • 📝Content: the data of every post. Popular posts sit in a hot cache.
  • 🕸️Social Graph: who follows whom (follower and following lists).
  • ❤️Action: whether a user liked, replied to or otherwise acted on a post.
  • 🔢Counters: counts of likes, replies, followers, following and so on.

Why split? Each layer has a different shape (lists, objects, sets, plain numbers), a different size and a different rhythm. Counters change all day long, while a post's text hardly ever does. Separate layers can each be sized, scaled and evicted (cleared out when memory runs low) on their own. Sort the 12 cards into their layers.

Watch one feed card get built. Each layer lights up as the card takes something from it.
In real life. Facebook's TAO is a whole system for one of these layers: it caches the social graph (people, posts and the links between them, like "follows" or "liked") in front of MySQL, across many data centers.[4] The author's name and avatar come from the user cache you used in stage 2; the book draws it in the retrieval figure rather than as one of the five layers.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

Hydration is a fan-out of its own. One feed page can touch the user cache, the post cache, the action layer and the counters. Real systems batch each one (multi-get) and run them in parallel, because a page is only as fast as its slowest lookup (its tail latency).

Facebook doesn't build feeds the book's way. Its Multifeed system doesn't keep a ready-made list per user. At read time it gathers recent actions from the people you follow out of sharded in-memory "leaf" servers, then ranks them.[3] The book's precomputed per-user list is closer to Twitter's timeline cache.

Counters at huge scale are often approximate. Millions of increments on one post's like counter hit one key, which is the same hot-key problem as celebrity fanout. Teams spread the increments across several keys and add them up, or update the count asynchronously, and accept that "1.2K" may lag a little.
