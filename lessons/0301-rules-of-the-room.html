1

Two Coworkers, One Whiteboard

What the interview is really simulating

The book frames the session as an imitation of real work: two colleagues tackle a vague problem together and talk their way to a solution that meets their goals.[1]

You'll be asked to "design" products that took hundreds or thousands of engineers years to build. Nobody expects that in under an hour. There's no single right answer, and the final diagram matters less than how you got there.

What matters is the signal: the evidence the interviewer writes down to justify "hire" or "no hire". The book lists five kinds: design skill, collaboration, working under pressure, resolving ambiguity (turning a fuzzy prompt into a concrete scope), and asking good questions.

2

Red Flag Detector

Over-engineering, narrow-mindedness, stubbornness

The book names three behaviours that sink candidates who otherwise know their stuff:[1]

  • Over-engineering: chasing a "pure", impressive design and ignoring trade-offs (the costs you accept for each benefit). The book says many engineers don't see how the costs of an over-built system pile up over time, and companies pay heavily for it.
  • Narrow-mindedness: locking onto one idea and refusing to look at alternatives.
  • Stubbornness: refusing to adjust when the interviewer raises a valid concern about your plan.

Careful: healthy behaviour can look similar. Defending a choice with a reason and then updating when new facts arrive is good. Choosing a simple design for a small scale is good judgment, not narrow-mindedness.

3

Budget the 45 Minutes

The four steps and their clocks

The book's framework has four steps, always in this order:[1]

  1. Understand the problem and set the design scope (what exactly are we building, for whom, at what size?)
  2. Propose a high-level design and get buy-in (a box diagram the interviewer agrees with)
  3. Deep dive into the parts that matter most
  4. Wrap up (bottlenecks, improvements, what you'd do next)

For a 45-minute session the book gives a rough guide: scope 3–10 min, high-level design 10–15 min, deep dive 10–25 min, wrap-up 3–5 min.

Dos & Don'ts Sorter

The chapter's checklist, in your own hands

The chapter ends with a list of dos and don'ts.[1] In short: clarify and never treat your own assumption as fact. Think out loud. Offer more than one approach. Give the big picture first, then detail the most critical parts. Treat the interviewer like a teammate. Ask for a hint when stuck. You're done when the interviewer says so, not when the boxes are drawn.

"Never give up" vs "ask for a hint". They don't clash. Asking for a hint after trying is fine and shows you collaborate. What hurts is a long, silent stall.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

The minute ranges are proportions, not law. In a 60-minute slot, scale them up, and keep the deep dive as the biggest block. The three red flags are the book's list; real rubrics differ by company and level, and many add things like communication clarity or "drives the discussion". At senior level, interviewers often expect you to set the agenda ("I'll spend a few minutes on requirements, then sketch, then go deep on the feed") rather than wait to be led. Finally, "never give up" and "ask for hints" fit together: try, think out loud, then ask. A long silent stall is the real failure mode.
