1

Pin your assumptions

Write them down before any math

An estimate is only as good as the numbers you feed it. So one of the book's tips is simple: write your assumptions down where the interviewer can see them.[1] Then both of you can check the math against them, and change one if it looks wrong.

🧑‍💼
InterviewerEstimate Twitter's QPS and storage requirements.
🙋
YouHere's what I'll assume: 300 million monthly users, half of them on it daily, 2 tweets each per day, 10% with a photo or video, and we keep everything 5 years. Does that sound reasonable?
🧑‍💼
InterviewerSounds reasonable. Go ahead.

Two terms you'll need. MAU (monthly active users) counts the different people who use the app at least once in a month. DAU (daily active users) counts them for one day. DAU is always a share of MAU.

Your move: eight facts are on the table. Only five of them change how many tweets arrive or how many bytes you store. Tap (or drag) those five onto the board.

📋 Assumptions0 / 5 pinned
In a real interview, you propose the numbers. Don't wait to be handed them. Say a number, say why, and ask "does that sound reasonable?" The book notes its own numbers are for practice only, not Twitter's real figures.[1] What gets graded is the method, not a perfect guess.

Crunch the QPS

Users → tweets per day → tweets per second → peak
📌 Pinned300M MAU50% daily2 tweets/user/day10% mediakeep 5 years

QPS means queries per second: how many requests hit the system each second. Here a "query" is one new tweet being written, so this is the write QPS. You get it in four small steps. Each step uses the answer from the one before.

Type a number and press Check (or Enter). Plain numbers are fine, and so are things like 150m or 3.5k.

⏱️ Seconds in a day (open if you need it)

24 hours × 60 minutes × 60 seconds = 86,400 seconds

That's close to 100,000 = 105. Dividing by 105 is just "drop five zeros", so 300 million ÷ 105 = 3,000. That's within about 15% of the exact answer, which is plenty for a napkin.

—tweets/s one made-up day
Why "× 2" for peak? Traffic isn't flat. People tweet more in the evening and during big events. The book just doubles the average.[1] That 2 is an assumption you state, not a law. Real spikes can be much bigger: in August 2013 Twitter hit 143,199 tweets per second during a TV broadcast in Japan, against a normal average of about 5,700.[2] That's about 25×.

Weigh the storage

The book sizes an average tweet like this:[1] a tweet_id of 64 bytes, 140 bytes of text, and 1 MB of media (a photo or short video) when the tweet has one. One megabyte is about 5,000 times bigger than the id and text put together (204 bytes).

Two piles: one day of tweet text, one day of media. Fill them in.
Book tip: label your units. "30" is meaningless. "30 TB" is an answer. Writing the unit at every step is how you catch a slip of 1,000× before the interviewer does.[1]

Mental-math trick: 365 × 5 is close to 2,000 (it's 1,825), so you can round first and fix up after.

Honest caveats. The book's 64-byte tweet_id is generous. Twitter's real IDs (Snowflake) are 64 bits, which is 8 bytes.[3] Tweets have allowed 280 characters since 2017, and in UTF-8 one character can take up to 4 bytes. None of that matters here: text stays tiny next to media.

Turn the knobs

What the interviewer asks next

The book lists the estimates interviewers ask for most: QPS, peak QPS, storage, cache, and number of servers.[1] After you give a number, the next question is usually "what if…?". The good news: every formula here is a chain of multiplications. So each knob moves the answer in proportion. Double a factor and the result doubles. Leave a factor out of a formula and it can't move that result at all.

What a senior adds that the book leaves out. Replication: real storage keeps about 3 copies of each file, so 55 PB becomes about 165 PB. Reads: people read timelines far more often than they post, so read QPS is many times write QPS. That's where caching comes in. Growth: 300 million users today won't be 300 million in year five. Mention one of these and you sound like you've run a real system.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

The book's example is deliberately narrow: writes and media only. A fuller answer adds four things. (1) Replication: about 3 copies, so roughly 165 PB. (2) Read traffic: timelines are read far more than they're written, so the read side drives your cache and server counts. (3) Metadata and indexes: small per tweet, but not zero. (4) Growth: users and media sizes rise over five years. The 2× peak rule is also a simplification. Twitter's 2013 record was about 25× its average,[2] so real systems plan for bursts with queues and spare capacity, not just a ×2 factor. The book points to the System Design Primer[4] for more practice estimates.
