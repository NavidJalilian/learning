The bouncers

Web servers: auth and rate limiting

The book's publishing deep dive (figure 11-4) follows one request. Ana's app sends POST /v1/me/feed. A load balancer hands it to a web server. The web servers then pass the post to three services: the post service (saves it), the fanout service (gets it into friends' feeds) and the notification service (pings friends).[1]

Web servers don't only talk to clients. They also run two checks before a post gets any further:

  • Authentication: only a signed-in user with a valid auth_token (the login pass the app sends with every request) may publish.
  • Rate limiting: each user may only post so many times in a time window. That stops spam and abuse. You built a whole rate limiter in World 4. Here it's just one box.

Five steps to every feed

The fanout workflow, in order

Ana's post got through the door. The web servers hand it to the fanout service, whose job is to get the new post's ID into every friend's feed. The book splits its work into five steps (figure 11-5). Two pieces of jargon first:

  • Graph database: a store where data is nodes (users) joined by edges (friendships). "Who are Ana's friends?" is one cheap hop along her edges. The book picks it because it's good at friend relationships and friend recommendations.[5]
  • Message queue: a buffer between two parts of a system. One side drops jobs in. Workers on the other side pick them up when they can.[4]

Not everyone gets it

Filtering by user settings

Step 2 isn't just a lookup. The fanout service filters the friend list using settings:[1]

  • Mute: if you mute a friend, their posts stay out of your feed, even though you're still friends.
  • Audience: a post can be shared with only some friends, or hidden from certain people.

Filtering at fanout time means the feed cache never holds a post that its owner shouldn't see, so reading a feed stays simple. Predict: tap every friend whose feed will get Ana's post, then press Fan out.

Why a queue in the middle?

Decouple the poster from the fanout

Steps 3 and 4 put a message queue and a pool of fanout workers between the fanout service and the feed cache. Ana gets "Posted!" as soon as her post is stored and the job is queued. Workers do the feed writes in the background. That split is called decoupling: the poster no longer waits for the slow part.

Now run it. Ana has 2,000 friends. Each worker writes 750 feed entries a second, and each post becomes 8 jobs of 250 writes. These are toy rates, chosen so you can watch it.

5

Store IDs, not posts

The feed cache diet

Step 5 writes into the news feed cache. The book describes it as a <post_id, user_id> mapping kept per viewer: one list for each user, saying which posts belong in their feed and who wrote them. Every fanned-out post adds one entry (figure 11-6).[1] Two rules keep it small:

  • IDs only. Copying full post and user objects into every friend's list would blow up memory: one post, copied hundreds of times.
  • A cap. Each list holds at most a configurable number of entries. Few people scroll through thousands of posts, and most want the newest ones, so cache misses stay rare.

    🎓 Level-up note (for later)

    • Whose user_id? The list is already keyed by the viewer, so the user_id in each entry is best read as the post's author. That's what you need to hydrate the feed later. Real systems often keep a capped Redis list (LPUSH + LTRIM) or a sorted set scored by time.[3]
    • "Graph DB" is a role, not a product. Facebook's social graph doesn't live in a classic graph database. It's TAO: a graph-shaped caching layer on top of sharded MySQL.[2] In the book, "graph DB" means "a store that's good at friend edges".
    • Fanout-time filters go stale. Unfriending, audience changes and deletions after fanout aren't reflected in entries already written. So reads re-check (Quest 11.4's hydration step).
    • At-least-once means duplicates. A queue normally re-delivers a job that wasn't acknowledged, so a retried job can write the same entry twice. Make feed writes idempotent (safe to repeat), for example a set keyed by post_id.[4]
    • Our numbers are illustrative. The 300 friends, worker rates, 16-byte entries and the scroll-depth curve are our assumptions, not the book's.
