Why transcode?

Four reasons, one calculator

In Quest 14.2, uploads went through a box called transcoding servers. Time to open it. Transcoding (also called video encoding) means converting a video into other formats: a different compression method, resolution or bitrate. The goal is a version that plays well on each device and network.[1]

Bitrate is how many bits the video uses per second of playback. More bits per second means more detail, but also more bandwidth to stream it and more computing power to play it.

Three numbers that people mix up. Truly raw 1080p60 for an hour is about 1.3 TB. The book says an hour of HD at 60 fps "can take up a few hundred GB". That matches a camera or editing master, which is lightly compressed (about 200 GB here). A streaming encode of the same hour is only a few GB. Transcoding is what gets you from the second number to the third.[1]

The other three reasons

Size is reason one. The book gives three more. Match each user story to the reason it shows.[1]

The four reasons, in one breath. Storage: raw video is huge. Compatibility: devices and browsers each play only some formats. Bandwidth: give fast connections high resolution and slow ones low resolution. Changing networks: the same viewer's connection rises and falls, so quality has to switch on the fly.

Box vs language

Containers and codecs

A video's encoding format has two parts, and the book keeps them apart:[1]

  • Container: the basket that holds the video stream, the audio stream and the metadata (title, length, subtitles…). The file extension tells you which one: .avi, .mov, .mp4.
  • Codec (coder-decoder): the compression and decompression method that shrinks the pictures while keeping them looking good. The book names H.264, VP9 and HEVC. A newer one, AV1, is now common too.

Think of the container as the box and the codec as the language the contents are written in. The same .mp4 box can hold H.264 or HEVC. To play a file, a device has to open the box and read the language.

Ride the bitrate ladder

Predict the stall, then drive the player

Reasons three and four need a trick called adaptive bitrate streaming (ABR).[3] Here's how it works:

  • The video is transcoded into a bitrate ladder: the same video at several quality levels ("rungs"), for example 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p and 4K.
  • Each rung is cut into short segments of a few seconds.
  • A manifest file lists every rung and where its segments live. HLS (Apple's protocol, one of the streaming protocols the book lists) calls it a playlist and uses .m3u8. MPEG-DASH uses .mpd.[4]
  • The player measures how fast segments arrive and picks the rung for each next segment.
# a cut-down HLS master playlist
#EXTM3U
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=700000,RESOLUTION=640x360
360p/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=5000000,RESOLUTION=1920x1080
1080p/index.m3u8
# …one entry per rung

The buffer is video that's already downloaded but not yet watched. It drains at one second per second while you watch. If it hits zero, you get a stall: the spinner.

Your train ride: the signal is 8 Mbit/s, then it drops to 0.8 Mbit/s in a tunnel from 20 s to 35 s. The clip is 60 seconds long, in 15 segments of 4 seconds. The player holds at most 12 seconds of buffer.

Wire the DAG

One upload, many tasks

Transcoding is slow and expensive. And creators want different things: some want a watermark, some upload their own thumbnail, some upload in HD and some don't. So the book doesn't hard-code one pipeline. It borrows the model from Facebook's streaming video engine (SVE): the work is a DAG, and programmers configure which tasks it contains.[1][2]

DAG = directed acyclic graph. Boxes are tasks. An arrow A → B means "A must finish before B starts". Acyclic means no loops. Tasks with no arrow between them can run at the same time on different machines.

The book's graph splits the original into video, audio and metadata. Then:

  • Inspection checks the video is good quality and not broken (malformed).
  • Video encodings make the versions: different resolutions, codecs and bitrates (360p.mp4 up to 4k.mp4).
  • Thumbnail: uploaded by the creator or generated automatically.
  • Watermark: an image laid over the video with identifying information.
  • Audio encoding handles the sound. At the end, everything is assembled into the encoded output.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

The book only names the streaming protocols. The mechanism that actually changes quality is ABR: renditions + segments + a manifest + a player algorithm. Real players mix two families of algorithm. Throughput-based ones pick a rung from measured download speed, like the auto player in stage 3. Buffer-based ones pick from how full the buffer is.[3]

One fixed ladder is also a simplification. Netflix tunes the ladder per title: a simple cartoon looks fine at a much lower bitrate than a grainy action film.[5] And YouTube now serves AV1 widely, alongside the book's H.264, VP9 and HEVC.
