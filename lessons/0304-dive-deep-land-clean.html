Dive Deep, Land Clean

You have a blueprint and a nod from the interviewer. Now spend the next 10 to 25 minutes where they count, skip the rabbit holes, and use the last few minutes to finish strong.

🎯

Your win today: choose and prioritise deep-dive topics with the interviewer, avoid low-signal rabbit holes, and deliver a wrap-up that names bottlenecks, failures, operations and the next scale step.

1

Where to Dig

Choosing deep-dive targets

Step 3, the design deep dive, gets about 10–25 minutes of a 45-minute interview.[1] By the time you get here you should already have:

  • agreed goals and feature scope (step 1),
  • a high-level blueprint (a box diagram of the main parts),
  • the interviewer's feedback on that blueprint,
  • a rough idea, from that feedback, of which areas to focus on.

Now you work with the interviewer to pick which components to dig into, and in what order. You don't go deep on everything. You pick the parts where the hard problems live.

Interviewers differ. Some, especially for senior roles, want a high-level talk about performance characteristics: where the bottlenecks are and how much hardware it needs. Most want you to dig into the details of a few components. The book's two examples: for a URL shortener, the hash function that turns a long URL into a short code. For a chat system, cutting latency and showing who's online.[1]

The Rabbit-Hole Trap

Spend minutes where the signal is

In step 3, time management is the skill. The book's warning: don't spend minutes on details that don't show you can design systems that scale.[1] Its example is Facebook's EdgeRank, the formula Facebook once used to decide which posts show first in your feed.[3] Explaining it in depth is interesting, but it says almost nothing about scale.

Think of it as signal per minute. "Signal" is the evidence the interviewer writes down to justify a hire. Cover the most critical components first.

The key idea in that deep dive is fan-out: getting Alice's new post into her friends' feeds. There are two ways to do it.[2]

  • Fan-out on write (push). When Alice posts, write the post's ID into each friend's ready-made feed in the cache. Reads are instant. Publishing costs one write per friend.
  • Fan-out on read (pull). Alice's post is written once. When Bob opens the app, his feed is built then, by fetching recent posts from everyone he follows. Publishing is cheap. Reads do the work.

The book's news feed lets a user have up to 5,000 friends, so one push can mean up to 5,000 cache writes.[2] Predict, then watch.

Land the Plane

Step 4: the last 3–5 minutes

Step 4, the wrap-up, gets 3–5 minutes. The interviewer may ask follow-ups, or let you choose what to add.[1] Good directions, from the book:

  • Bottlenecks and improvements. Never say the design is perfect. There's always something to improve, and spotting it shows critical thinking.
  • A short recap, which matters most if you weighed several options along the way.
  • Error cases: a server fails, the network drops.
  • Operations: how you'd watch metrics and error logs, and how you'd roll the system out.[5]
  • The next scale step: if this handles 1 million users, what changes at 10 million?
  • Other refinements you'd make with more time.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

EdgeRank is history. It was Facebook's early, publicly described ranking formula. Facebook moved to machine-learned ranking years ago.[3] The book uses it only as an example of a low-signal rabbit hole, not as how feeds are ranked today.

Push vs pull is a preview. Chapter 11 covers it properly, including the hybrid approach and the cache tiers. Real feeds also pull celebrity posts at read time and merge them in.

Follow the interviewer's lead. Some interviewers deliberately steer the deep dive somewhere you didn't plan, to see how you handle it. Following them beats sticking to your plan.

For the operations answer, Google's SRE book names four "golden signals" to watch on any service: latency, traffic, errors and saturation (how full it is).[5] For rollout, "release gradually, with a quick way back" is the core of its release-engineering chapter.[6]
