The Estimation Gauntlet

One prompt, one interviewer, ten minutes on the clock: "Estimate the scale of a Twitter-like service." Everything from Quests 2.1–2.4 at once, and nobody tells you which number comes next.

🎯

Your win today: given "estimate the scale of a Twitter-like service", you can ask for the right assumptions, lay out the estimation plan, compute write/read QPS, storage, cache and server counts with units, handle "what if" follow-ups, and say what the numbers mean for the design.

Scope it

Step 1 · ask for the numbers you need (2–3 min)

An estimate starts like any design question: clarify before you compute. The book's framework says Step 1 is asking questions, not answering them[2]. Here that means asking for the inputs your formulas need: users, activity, sizes, how long you keep things.

The clock bar above counts game time. Each good question costs 15 seconds. A question that doesn't change the numbers costs 15 seconds too, and earns nothing.

Plan the math

Step 2 · lay out what you'll compute, in order

The book lists the estimates interviewers ask for most: QPS (queries per second), peak QPS, storage, cache and number of servers[1]. Say the plan before you compute. It gets the interviewer's buy-in, just like Step 2 of the framework, and it shows structure.

Tap the eight estimates in an order that works: each one must come after the numbers it uses. There's more than one right order. An out-of-order tap costs 10 seconds.

Crunch it

Step 3 · numbers on the board, with units

Work down the whiteboard. Type a number, pick its unit, check it. You can type 150m, 3.5k or 1.5e9. Close is what counts here, not exact:

  • Green: within ±30% of the model answer, unit labeled. Full XP.
  • Amber: the right order of magnitude (within 3×), or no unit. Half XP.
  • Red: further off. Costs a heart and 20 seconds. One slip is charged once: if a later row is off only because it used your own earlier wrong number with the right method, that row is amber, not red.

Each row takes 25 seconds. A hint shows the formula and costs 15 seconds more. Tip: 86,400 seconds in a day is close to 100,000, so "÷ 86,400" is roughly "drop five zeros" (Quest 2.1).

Boss: the follow-up barrage

Step 3 · "what if…?" (latency, nines, and changing numbers)

Now the interviewer pokes the estimate to see if you can reuse it. Seven follow-ups, pulling in latency numbers (Quest 2.2), availability nines (Quest 2.3) and the "turn one knob" rule (Quest 2.4). Each answer takes 15 seconds; a wrong one costs a heart and 20 seconds more.

Honest caveats from the barrage. (1) Series availability math (multiply the percentages) assumes the services fail independently. Shared failures, such as one bad deploy or one region outage, make the real number worse[6]. (2) The latency figures are the book's classic table; today's hardware is faster for disks and SSDs, but a round trip across an ocean is still bounded by the speed of light[4].

Wrap it up

Step 4 · what the numbers mean for the design

An estimate only earns its keep if it changes a decision. The interviewer's real question after any number is "so what?"

Link each number to the design move it forces: tap a number, then its move

A wrong link costs a heart the first time for each number. More misses on the same number only cost time.

Build your closing statement: one sentence per slot
Honest caveat. How many writes one database primary can take varies hugely with hardware, schema and indexes. "A few thousand writes per second per primary" is a rough guide to say out loud, not a fact. That's why ~7,000 at peak, growing for five years, is a reason to plan for sharding (splitting the data across several databases), not proof you need it on day one.

🎓 Level-up note

  • The book's chapter is a toolkit, not a "design X" prompt. This boss stretches its Twitter example into a fuller estimate (reads, cache, servers). The book lists those as common asks but doesn't compute them.
  • The 10 timeline loads a day, ~1,000 requests/s per app server and ~200 bytes per cached tweet are interviewer-given practice assumptions, not facts. Real per-server throughput runs from hundreds to tens of thousands of requests per second, depending on the work each request does.
  • The 2× peak factor is a simplification. Twitter's 2013 record was about 25× its average[5], so real systems absorb bursts with queues, autoscaling and spare capacity.
  • 3 full replicas is the simple answer. Big blob stores often use erasure coding (math-based parity, like RAID across machines) to get similar safety for roughly 1.5× the raw size instead of 3×.
  • Practise this one out loud with a timer. Clicking the right number is not the same as producing it under pressure.
