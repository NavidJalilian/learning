Step 1 · understand the problem (3–10 min)

Every system design interview in the book has the same four-step shape[2]. This prompt is unusual: it isn't "design X", it's "make this bigger". So scoping is about the shape of the load: how many users, how much reading versus writing, how much is images, where people live, and what's slow.

The chapter's order is an order of pain: each new component fixes the symptom that shows up next[1]. Nine alarms are coming. For each one, read the dashboard, pick the fix from the toolbox, then answer the interviewer's "and if that breaks?" follow-up.

Boss: the curveball barrage

Step 3 · "what happens when…?"

Your design is on the board. Now the interviewer pokes holes in it. Eight curveballs, and each answer is one of four moves. Name the kind of fix before you name a product.

Honest caveats. Caching across regions is harder than it looks: keeping the cache and the database in sync is a real research problem. Facebook's memcache paper is the classic deep read on it[4]. And "promote a replica" sounds instant, but deciding the master is really dead (not just slow) and stopping two masters from both taking writes is the hard part in practice.

🎓 Level-up note

Pain first, boxes second. A senior answer never lists components. It says which number broke (CPU, read load, latency far away, session loss, outage blast radius, request time, disk) and only then which box fixes it. Three honest gaps in the book's story: (1) a single big database goes much further than people expect (Stack Overflow ran on one master in 2013), so sharding is a last resort, not a milestone; (2) a second data center doubles your cost and forces you to decide what happens to writes during a split between regions, which is why many companies put it off; (3) the "split into services" rule pays off for team independence more than for raw scale.
