1

Treat Them Like a Teammate

Blueprint first, then ask

This is step 2 of the book's four-step framework: propose a high-level design and get buy-in. Budget: about 10–15 minutes of a 45-minute interview.[1]

The goal has two halves. First, a blueprint: a rough first plan of the main parts, drawn as boxes and arrows. Second, buy-in: the interviewer agrees it's a sensible base to build on. The book's advice is to work with the interviewer. Propose, ask for feedback, and treat them like a teammate. Many good interviewers enjoy joining in.[1]

It's minute 9. In Quest 3.2 you scoped a news feed: mobile and web, users post and see friends' posts newest first, up to 5,000 friends, 10 million daily active users, posts can hold photos and video. How do you start?

Standard building blocks

The book suggests drawing box diagrams of the key parts. It lists the usual suspects: clients (mobile or web), APIs, web servers, data stores, a cache, a CDN and a message queue.[1] Most designs reuse the same handful of boxes, so learn what each one is for.

3

Two Flows of a Feed

Feed publishing and feed building

The book's step-2 example splits the news feed into two flows:[1]

  • Feed publishing. A user posts. The post is saved to a cache and a database, then pushed into each friend's news feed.
  • News feed building. The feed is made by gathering friends' posts, newest first (reverse chronological order).

Two new boxes here. The fanout service "fans out" one post to many feeds, one per friend. The news feed cache stores each user's ready-made feed as a list of post IDs. Build each flow: tap a lane to choose it, then tap boxes in the order a request travels.

4

Pick the Zoom Level

API endpoints and schema: yes or no?

Should step 2 include API endpoints (the exact calls a client makes, like GET /v1/me/feed) and a database schema (the tables and their columns)? The book's answer: it depends on the problem.[1]

For a huge prompt like "design Google search", that's too low-level for step 2. For a small one like the backend of a multiplayer poker game, it's fair game. Either way, talk it over with the interviewer.

Set the dial for each prompt. Spot on: full XP. One notch off: half.

5

Boss: Pushback at Minute 15

The interviewer pokes the blueprint

Your box diagram is on the board. Now the interviewer reacts. For each comment, pick your move: adjust the blueprint, defend it with a short reason, park the detail for the deep dive (step 3), or ask a clarifying question. Win at least 4 of 6.

🎓 Level-up note (for later)

The chapter's figures are deliberately high-level. How fan-out works is a Chapter 11 topic: push the post into feeds when it's written ("fanout on write"), or gather it when someone reads ("fanout on read"), or mix the two so a celebrity with millions of followers doesn't trigger millions of writes. You only got a preview here.[2]

The CDN and object storage for video are our addition, following the "posts can hold video" requirement. The chapter's step-2 figures only show the two flows.

Rough estimation in step 2 is optional. Some interviewers skip it, others insist on it, so ask first (the maths itself is in Ch. 2[3]). And the "cache in front of the database" box hides a lot of real engineering: Facebook's paper on running memcache at scale shows what it takes to keep such a cache consistent and fast.[4]
